# P9 research, part 3: the planner: where words go, when they start, the re-check, the mix

The planner of P7 ([ADR 100](../adr/100-knowledge-model-fsrs-planner-and-answer-log.md), point 2) picks a kana and a task for it: a due review, a learning step whose time has come, an introduction, or a learning step that waits. A learning kana is recognition one time in four and recall otherwise.

P9 adds words and new task types:

- [ADR 116](../adr/116-words-written-in-kana-and-varied-tasks-in-p9.md) names the task types and asks that they be interleaved, so that a session is not one kind of task after another.
- [ADR 117](../adr/117-grades-of-the-p9-task-types.md) grades them. A word is one recall per kana, and words may hold learning kana.

This part answers the questions the two ADRs left to it:

- where a word goes in a session and how many a session holds;
- when words start and how often a word comes back;
- when the kana of an error is checked again;
- the closing word;
- the threshold of rule errors;
- how the other task types enter the mix.

This note decides nothing and changes neither the model nor the app. The numbers come from `packages/sim` at the commit of this note (`pnpm --filter @nekotype/sim run p9-planner`, one table per `PART`). Each case runs the production model through `createProductionPolicy` with 50 beginners over 60 days on hiragana. The words are the 1,074 hiragana words of two kana or more of [part 1](2026-11-27-p9-words-content.md), graded as ADR 117 says. A cell is a median.

## In short

- **Words go between the kana tasks, about one after every 5 of them.** That is about 16% of the tasks. The planner's own choice of kana stays as it is.
  - The pace is the same as without words (the 46 basic kana are read on day 7, the track on day 13). The retention of reviews is 93.2%.
  - There are 15% fewer reviews.
  - The learner knows about 3 more kana after a 30-day break.
  - A word after every 4 to 7 tasks gives the same. After every 2 or 3 it slows the kana (part 2).
- **A word should not take the place of a recall.** In the strongest variant a word that holds the kana asked replaces the recall, which is 35% of the recalls when such a word exists. The track is then read on day 11, with 35% fewer reviews than without words and 23% fewer than with words between tasks.
  - **That gain rests on an assumption the simulator cannot check:** that reading a kana inside a word helps its memory at least 0.6 times as much as recalling it alone.
  - **At 0.3 the carrier fails.** With half the recalls replaced, the retention of reviews falls to 86% (the goal is 90%), the track is read on day 18, and on day 14 the learner knows 64 kana instead of 102.
  - Words between tasks keep 92.7% at 0.3. The safe rule is the one that adds words, not the one that replaces recalls.
- **When words start does not matter to the model.** It matters to what the learner reads. At a threshold of 1 or 10 readable words, words start in the first session with the vowels, mostly interjections (ああ, いいえ). At 25 they start in session 4, still on day 1, with the k row (かき, かっこいい, おいしい), at no cost. The proposal is 25.
- **A word read in the last 30 words is not given again.** That takes the words read twice on the same day from 4.3% to 0.2%, at no cost. A word read again soon is more likely read whole, which is what part 2 warned of.
- **The re-check after an error is the model's own learning step.** An error puts the kana in learning, and it comes back 30 seconds later, about 7 tasks. A recall added 1, 2, 3 or 5 tasks after the error changes nothing measurable in the simulator.
  - What the screen promises ("it comes back later in this session", decision 152) is kept by a round after the session's last task. The maintainer proposed it in place of a longer session ([Slips and the round after a session](#table-5-slips-and-the-round-after-a-session)).
- **A slip in a recall should be "hard", not "again".** A slip is one rejected key that matches no kana, after which the learner types the kana right without a hint. Today it counts as a recall error. Under an assumed 3% of slips on recalls of known kana, that leaves the learner knowing 89 kana on day 14 instead of 103, and the track is read on day 15 instead of 13. At 6% it is 65 kana and day 22. Read as "hard", slips cost nothing.
- **The round after a session:** each kana answered wrong in the session comes back once as a recall, at most 5. It speeds the track by one day (day 12 instead of 13) at no cost, and it adds about 0.2 tasks to an average session.
- **The closing word costs nothing:** 35 more words over 60 days and the same pace. It is the last task of a session, drawn from the words whose kana were all in that session.
- **Two rule errors in a row is the right threshold.** In katakana, where 46% of the words hold っ or ー, a threshold of one would show the rule again about every 4 sessions to a learner who knows it and makes one slip in ten. Two in a row brings that to about once in 50 sessions. A learner who does not know the rule still sees it again within about 3 uses. Three in a row would take 5 to 14 uses.
- **The other task types enter by rules, not by a lottery.** Each takes the task of a kana the planner has already picked, under a condition the model can check (the list below). Interleaving then follows from the cadence of words and from these conditions. No separate rule "no two of a kind in a row" is needed.

## Changes to the simulator

They are opt-in options of `packages/sim`. Every table of parts 1 and 2 reproduces byte for byte, and the sim's tests pass.

- **`WordOptions` of `createProductionPolicy`:**
  - `every` (part 2's cadence) may now be absent;
  - `carrier`: the share of the planner's recalls given as a word that holds the kana asked, when one is readable;
  - `minPool`: no word before this many words are readable at the start of a session;
  - `recent`: a word among the last N given is not given again;
  - `closing`: the last task of a session is a word whose kana were all in the session.
- **`ProductionOptions.recheck`:** after a wrong answer, the kana comes back as a recall N tasks later in the same session, beside the model's own step.
- **`RunResult.wordRepeats`** (words given again on a day they were already read) and **`firstWordSession`**.
- **`ReferenceParams.slip`:** the chance that a recall the learner knows has one slip, a rejected key that matches no kana. It is 0 by default, with no draw, so earlier tables reproduce. `ProductionOptions.slips` (`error` or `hard`) says how the fold hears of a slip, and `ProductionOptions.endRound` is the round after a session.
- **`src/p9-planner.ts`** prints the tables (`PART=place|start|recheck|closing|round`).

- **A carried recall is cheaper.** It needs no task of its own, and the other kana of the word are practised for free. That is where its gain comes from: the track on day 11, and 886 reviews instead of 1,153.
- **The gain is also its risk.** The model counts each kana of the word as a full recall. If reading in a word gives less than that, the model believes kana are stronger than they are. It then schedules fewer reviews, and they fail.
- **Words between tasks cannot fail that way.** They add practice on top of the planner's own recalls, so even an over-counted read only moves a review later by a little.
- **How much reading a kana in a word gives is not measured anywhere for kana.** Choosing the carrier would be a bet. It can come back after the release, if the answer log of real learners shows that a kana read in words holds as well as a recalled one.

The minutes per day rise from 8.0 to about 9 with words. A sitting lasts as long as before. A word takes longer than a kana, and the harness lets the last session of a sitting finish.

- **The threshold moves the first word by a few sessions, all on day 1, and changes nothing else.** What it changes is what the first words are. With words of introduced kana, the vowels already give 12 words of part 1's selection. Most are interjections, and one is a plant name hardly anyone knows (あい, the dyer's knotweed). The k row brings 25 with っ and ー, the first common nouns and adjectives among them.
- **Not giving a word again within the last 30** costs nothing, because the pool grows faster than the words are read. Early, when the pool is small, it may leave a session with no word. Then the cadence waits for a readable one, as it already does.
- **Words with っ or ー** come only after their rule's introduction (ADR 117, point 3). The simulator does not model rules, and in it っ and ー are skipped.

The model alone puts a kana answered wrong back in learning, and it comes back after its step of 30 seconds. Here a recall is added N tasks after the error as well.

- **An extra re-check changes nothing measurable.** The simulated learner gains almost nothing from a recall seconds after the answer was shown. That matches the evidence: the benefit of feedback after an error comes from seeing the right answer, not from testing it again at once (Pashler et al. 2005).
- **The model's step already comes "a few tasks later".** 30 seconds is about 7 tasks of 4 seconds (`TASK_DEFAULT_MS`). That fits the methods note: "not as the next task".
- **What remains is a promise of the screen.** After an error the notice line says the kana comes back later in this session (decision 152). An error near the end of a session would break it.
  - A first proposal was a session that does not end while a kana answered wrong in it still waits for its step, up to 5 tasks past `SESSION_TASKS`.
  - The maintainer proposed instead that a session keep its length and be followed by a round that checks each kana answered wrong once. Table 5 measures that round.

## The threshold of rule errors

A rule (っ or ー) is shown again after k rule errors in a row, and a use with the rule right resets the count (ADR 117, point 3). With a chance q of an error on each use, the expected number of uses until k errors in a row is (1 − qᵏ) / ((1 − q) qᵏ).

| errors in a row | slip of a learner who knows the rule | uses until a needless re-show | error of a learner who does not | uses until it is shown again |
| --------------- | ------------------------------------ | ----------------------------- | ------------------------------- | ---------------------------- |
| 1               | 0.1                                  | 10                            | 0.5                             | 2                            |
| 1               | 0.2                                  | 5                             | 0.8                             | 1.2                          |
| 2               | 0.05                                 | 420                           | 0.5                             | 6                            |
| 2               | 0.1                                  | 110                           | 0.5                             | 6                            |
| 2               | 0.2                                  | 30                            | 0.8                             | 2.8                          |
| 3               | 0.1                                  | 1110                          | 0.5                             | 14                           |
| 3               | 0.2                                  | 155                           | 0.8                             | 4.8                          |

How often a rule is used follows from the cadence:

- At about 5 words a session, a rule comes up about 0.5 times a session in hiragana, where 11% of the selected words hold っ or ー. In katakana, where 46% do, it comes up about 2.3 times.
- With k = 1 and a slip of 0.1, a katakana learner who knows the rules sees one again about every 4 sessions.
- With k = 2 that becomes about once in 50, and a learner who errs half the time still sees it again within about 3 katakana sessions.
- In hiragana, where っ is rarer, it takes about 12 sessions. Words with the rule right after its introduction shorten that, and they are needed anyway to practise it.
- k = 3 is too slow to catch a rule not understood.

The proposal is k = 2. After the introduction of a rule, and after it is shown again, the next 2 word tasks are words with it.

## The other task types in the mix

The planner keeps choosing the kana as it does: due, ready, introduction, waiting. A P9 task type takes the task of the kana already chosen when its condition holds. The model can check each condition from its state, and none needs a lottery beyond today's share of recognition.

| task type             | takes the task of                                    | when                                                                                                | at most                       |
| --------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------- |
| word                  | — (between kana tasks)                               | after every 5 kana tasks, once 25 words are readable, not one of the last 30 words                  | about 16% of tasks            |
| closing word          | the last task                                        | a readable word whose kana were all in the session                                                  | 1 a session                   |
| rule introduction     | the next word task                                   | before the first readable word that needs the rule; again after 2 rule errors in a row              | 1 a session                   |
| transfer (か → カ)    | the introduction and the recognitions of a katakana  | its hiragana is learned                                                                             | as recognition today (1 in 4) |
| build                 | the introduction and the recognitions of a composite | its parts are learned (the model already waits for that)                                            | as recognition today          |
| contrast              | a recognition                                        | the kana has a confusion count of at least `WEAK_CONFUSIONS` (2) with an introduced kana of its set | as recognition today          |
| find the error        | a review recall                                      | the kana has such a confusion and a readable word holds it                                          | 1 a session                   |
| fluency run           | one slot in the middle of the session                | at least 8 learned kana are accurate (`WEAK_ACCURACY`) and slower than their pace                   | 1 a session, about 20 s       |
| hiragana or katakana? | a recognition                                        | both tracks have learned kana                                                                       | 1 a session                   |

- **Transfer, build and contrast** take the place of recognition, so they do not change how much of a session is choice and how much is recall. Recall keeps three tasks in four, as now. The simulator has none of their effects on memory, so this rule is argued, not measured: the grades of ADR 117 keep each of them at most a hard review, as recognition is.
- **Find the error, the fluency run and "hiragana or katakana?"** are at most one a session. They are variety and targeted practice, and none of them introduces kana. A fluency run takes about 20 seconds of a sitting of about 5 minutes.
- **The interleaving ADR 116 asks for follows from these rules.** With a word after every 5 kana tasks and a non-recall task for one learning task in four, recall never runs more than 5 tasks in a row while a word is readable. A rule against two tasks of a kind in a row would only fight the planner's choice of kana.
- The numbers `WEAK_CONFUSIONS` (2) and `WEAK_ACCURACY` (0.8) are the model's own definitions of a weak kana for the home (P8). Using them keeps "weak" meaning one thing.

## Table 5: slips and the round after a session

The maintainer asked for the round on 2026-11-27. A session stays at its length, and right after it a round begins. In the round each kana answered wrong in the session comes back once, to tell an error of haste from one of memory.

**What tells haste from forgetting.** It is the error itself, not the round. After Esc, or after the feedback that teaches, the learner has just seen the right answer, so a right answer in the round a minute later shows that the answer was seen, not that the kana was known. A rejected key shows nothing: the learner goes on typing. Within the task, then, the evidence is plain.

| what happened in the task                                                               | reading        | grade                     | in the round |
| --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------- | ------------------------- | ------------ |
| one key that matches no kana, then the kana typed right without a hint, within its pace | haste (a slip) | hard                      | no           |
| the romaji of another kana typed (シ → `tsu`)                                           | confused       | "again" and the confusion | yes          |
| Esc, two or more errors, or the right input after a slip but slower than the pace       | not remembered | "again"                   | yes          |

This is ADR 117's rule for a fluency run, applied to recall. The difference is that a forgiven slip is "hard" rather than no update, because a recall must still tell the model something.

**The simulator** gives the learner a chance of a slip on a recall of a kana they know (`slip`): one rejected key and 400 ms more, with the memory of a right recall. The policy reads a slip as today, a wrong recall (`slips: 'error'`), or as a right recall with a hint, which the fold grades "hard" (`slips: 'hard'`). The round (`endRound`) gives each kana answered wrong in the session (a forgiven slip is not wrong) once as a recall, in the order of the first errors, at most 5. Words after every 5 kana tasks start after 25 words and are not repeated within 30, as proposed above.

- **Slips read as errors are the costliest thing measured in these three parts.** Each slip sends a known kana back to its learning steps. It then needs 5 correct recalls in a row before it is learned again, and it holds a place among the 5 kana learning at once, so introductions wait.
  - The model reviews more, and the retention of reviews rises to 95–97% because the reviews are easy.
  - The learner meanwhile falls behind: 89 or 65 kana on day 14 instead of 103.
- **Read as "hard", slips cost nothing.** The learner knows the kana, and the model is told it was right with some trouble.
- **The rate of slips is an assumption.** Nobody has measured it for this application. The simulator only shows that the cost grows fast with it, while forgiving a slip costs nothing at any rate.
- **The round speeds the track by one day** (the 46 basic kana read on day 6 instead of 7, the track on day 12 instead of 13) and costs nothing in retention or in time. It adds about 0.2 tasks to an average session, because most sessions end with few errors.
  - A round of all the errors gives the same as a round of at most 5. The cap of 5 keeps a hard session from being followed by a second one.
  - With slips read as errors, the round recovers part of the loss (97 instead of 89 kana), but not all.
- **"Tasks a session" is about 18.5, not 30.** A session also ends when the planner has nothing to offer, which is common while the learning kana wait for their steps. The round then follows that shorter session as it would a full one.
